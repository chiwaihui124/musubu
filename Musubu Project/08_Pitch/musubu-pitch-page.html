Musubu · むすぶ · to tie together

Japan's empty towns are not a preference problem. They're a coordination failure.

Everyone who might move is waiting to see whether anyone else will — and nobody moves alone. Musubu applies a threshold mechanic to migration itself. Families, employers, towns and builders each declare a conditional commitment, and they all resolve at the same moment. A community exists before anyone has to arrive into an empty one.

This page is not a fundraise. It's a finished project looking for a team — and a seat on it.

Working prototype · Bungotakada, Oita — two hours from Fukuoka

Every tile is something that doesn't exist yet, and will if enough people say yes.

Click a pin. Then drag the timeline and watch the town fill in — first with only what's actually committed, then with what happens if the open thresholds are met.

The insight

Two moves, and the second one is the product.

01

Relocation is a state, not a trait.

People don't move because they prefer the countryside. They move because their life is already in motion — a first child, a breakup, burnout, kids leaving, retirement. At those moments the costs of moving are already being paid for other reasons. You aren't persuading anyone to uproot; you're catching people already mid-air and offering a better landing. The addressable population is enormous, and it renews every year.

02

What Musubu sells can't be bought anywhere else.

The last time most adults joined a group where everyone arrived at once and nobody was already inside was university. That's why those years are the most friendship-dense of most people's lives — not youth, but the absence of an established group to break into. Every adult move after twenty-two is the opposite, which is why relocating is lonely, and why "move to the countryside" fails for people who genuinely wanted it.

Musubu manufactures freshman year on demand, for adults, at any life stage. It's the one good that can't exist without coordination — which is exactly why nobody sells it.

Why now

The mechanic is old. The layer underneath it isn't.

Escrow and progress bars are 2009 technology. What was missing is the ability to understand thousands of people well enough to propose a specific life to each of them.

Surveys could only ever return what their authors had already imagined, because every question had to be pre-coded into options. That ceiling is gone. Open questions are now askable at national scale — which means the map of what actually stops people can emerge from what they say, rather than being invented in advance by someone in a meeting.

It also means a very small team can do what recently needed a large one. Which is the operative fact for anyone deciding whether to start this.

Why Kyushu

A Kyushu problem with a Kyushu test site.

Bungotakada is roughly two hours from Fukuoka. Fukuoka is Japan's startup city by explicit policy — a national strategic special zone, with a startup visa scheme it pioneered. Regional revitalization is a funded national priority, not a niche cause.

And Fukuoka Growth Next occupies a converted former elementary school. A project about turning closed buildings back into living ones, pitched inside a startup hub that is itself a revived closed school, is not a coincidence worth wasting.

How Musubu earns

Unglamorous near-term, government-funded, and aligned with the only metric that matters.

Municipality · near term

Coordination contract per pilot. Towns already hold revitalization budget and currently spend it on brochures and individual relocation grants with poor retention.

Municipality · near term

Retention-linked placement fee. Paid per household still there at twelve months — not per household moved. The incentive points at the thing that actually decides whether any of this worked.

Employers, developers · medium term

Coordination and housing margin. Company relocations, project-cohort staffing, renovation project management.

Ministries, employers · the ceiling

The intent-and-blocker dataset. Every ministry has heard that people would consider leaving Tokyo; that isn't new and it doesn't move budget. What doesn't exist is a ranked, segmented, annually refreshed map of what precisely stops them — and policy is written against blockers, not desires.

A meaningful share of early cost is plausibly grant-funded: the project has a legitimate simultaneous claim on reskilling, regional revitalization and cultural preservation budgets, because it genuinely does all three. For an investor that means less dilution and longer runway than the sector norm.

The ask

Not money. A team.

A startup studio or founding team that wants to adopt this. An existing company in adjacent territory — property tech, mobility, regional revitalization — that wants it as a line of business. Or a funded founder looking for a thesis, who wants a strategist alongside them.

What's offered is the entire corpus, the prototype, the pilot research, and continued work — with no claim to control.

First moves

Four things, none of which one person should be attempting alone.

Each needs Japanese fluency, local presence, or institutional standing. That's the whole argument for bringing this to people rather than trying to carry it.

  1. Get a renovation labour cost from a Bungotakada contractor. One conversation, in Japanese. It gates the sweat-equity model, the housing economics and the threshold size — and it's currently the one estimate sitting inside an otherwise evidenced argument.
  2. Native review and live testing of the Japanese intake instrument, then eight to ten real respondents.
  3. First contact with Bungotakada city. There's a kindergarten on the map marked 休園中 — suspended. Confirming its status is a genuine reason to call, and doubles as an opening.
  4. Approach the share-house operator with the deal shape that requires no capital from either side.
