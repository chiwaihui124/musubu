# Build Partnership Model
## Oak House, Sweat Equity, and the Carpenter Register

*Written August 2026. Answers the renovation-cost problem flagged in `04_Pilot_Towns/bungotakada_tile_candidates.md`. Companion to `musubu_housing_coordination_strategy.md`.*

---

## Summary

Three things that look separate and are actually one system:

1. **An Oak House partnership** — because their tenants already *are* the cohort, and their rotation mechanic converts "move to the countryside" into "transfer within the network."
2. **A build-while-you-live programme** — because it turns the largest cost line in the model (renovation labour) into the strongest recruitment tool.
3. **A national carpenter register** — because supervision is what makes the programme lawful, and because Japan's 大工 are aging out with no successors.

And the loop they close: **each cohort trains the next cohort's builders.**

---

## 1. The Oak House Partnership

### What each side actually has

| Musubu has | Oak House has |
|---|---|
| Demand-side coordination and the threshold mechanic | Operational competence at running shared housing in Japan |
| Rural akiya access at near-zero acquisition cost | A tenant base that *is* the target segment |
| Government and municipal relationships | Brand trust with mobile 20s–30s, including non-Japanese residents |
| The map, the profile, the mission narrative | Leases, guarantor-free entry, multilingual support, dispute handling |
| Cohort formation before anyone moves | Proven ability to fill rooms |

### The unlock is rotation, not property

Oak House's existing product mechanic is that a tenant can **transfer between properties in the network**. Point that at a rural akiya and the entire psychological structure of the decision changes:

> "Move to the countryside" — irreversible, identity-threatening, socially costly
> becomes
> "Transfer to another house in the network for three months" — same contract you already signed

That is an enormous de-risking, and it costs Oak House nothing to offer, because it is what they already do. It is also the `project_cohort` and circulating-labour model from `musubu_coordination_architecture.md`, delivered by a partner who already runs the logistics.

### How to pitch them honestly

**Do not pitch "rural share houses are a great market."** They would know it's false — lower rents, harder fill, thinner margins. That pitch fails on contact.

**Pitch the network argument instead:**

> A rural property that Osaka tenants can rotate into makes the *Osaka* property more valuable.

Supported by:
- **Near-zero acquisition cost** — akiya at ¥1M–4.8M against urban Japanese property
- **Musubu carries the demand risk** — cohorts are assembled before the property opens, so Oak House isn't holding fill risk on an unproven geography
- **A brand and story asset** — the rural house is the thing that gets written about, and it markets the whole network
- **A growth path around their ceiling** — urban acquisition is expensive and getting worse

### Three deal shapes

| | Structure | Oak House capital | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A · Operator agreement** | Municipality or Musubu owns; Oak House operates for a fee | None | Scaling once demand is proven |
| **B · Network affiliate** ★ | Independently owned and run, listed in the Oak House network, transfers honoured | None | **Starting.** Costs them almost nothing and tests the rotation demand directly |
| **C · Joint venture flagship** | One property, shared investment and brand | Meaningful | Later, once B has data |

**Recommendation: start at B.** It is the smallest thing that tests the actual hypothesis — will urban share-house tenants rotate into a rural property — and it requires no capital commitment from either side. Consistent with the "size the first threshold to be finishable" rule in `musubu_coordination_architecture.md` §6.

## 2. Build While You Live

### The economics

Renovation labour is the dominant cost line, and the largest unverified number in the entire model. Sweat equity attacks it directly — and does something better than reduce it.

**It converts the biggest cost into the biggest recruitment tool.** People pay to learn carpentry. Here they are housed while learning it, and the thing they build is somewhere a person will live.

### Worked model

*Using real Bungotakada figures. **The labour-share assumption is the load-bearing unknown and must be verified with a local contractor.***

| | Conventional | Sweat-equity build |
|---|---|---|
| Akiya purchase | ¥2,000,000 | ¥2,000,000 |
| Renovation — materials | ¥3,300,000 | ¥3,300,000 |
| Renovation — labour | ¥2,700,000 | — |
| Master carpenter fees | — | ¥900,000 |
| Trainee stipends + housing | — | ¥600,000 |
| **Project cost** | **¥8,000,000** | **¥6,800,000** |
| *Less training subsidy* | — | *potentially ¥600K–900K* |

> Assumes ¥6M conventional renovation with labour at 45%. Both figures need verification: the ¥3–8M renovation range in `musubu_housing_coordination_strategy.md` is itself unsourced, and the labour share is my estimate.

### But cost-per-house is the wrong metric

A conventional renovation buys you **a house**.

A sweat-equity renovation buys you **a house, plus a trained person who is now socially embedded in the town and materially more likely to stay.**

The right measure is **cost per retained resident**, and on that basis the comparison isn't close. Retention is the metric the entire policy argument rests on (`musubu_coordination_architecture.md` §6), and this programme is the strongest retention mechanism in the model.

### What the trainee gets
- Housing during the build — in the house they're working on, or the one next door
- A stipend or training allowance, potentially subsidised
- A certified, portable skill
- A claim: an option on the finished unit, a discount, or priority on the next one
- Twelve strangers who spent six months building something together

### Not everything can be sweat equity

**Trainees can do:** demolition, insulation, flooring, interior carpentry, joinery, plaster, painting, landscaping, finish work.

**Licensed trades must do:** structural work, electrical, plumbing, gas, roofing where safety-critical.

This is a design constraint, not a footnote — it's *why* the carpenter register is structural rather than decorative. Supervision is what makes the programme lawful.

### It is also the best social engine in the model

You could not design a better one. Twelve strangers renovating a house together for six months delivers, simultaneously: side-by-side contact, shared purpose, visible daily progress, and an unavoidable reason to talk to each other — all four mechanisms from `musubu_transition_thesis.md` §"Four Social Engines" in a single activity.

## 3. The Carpenter Register

### Rotating specialists, not one master per build

A carpenter comes for **six weeks to lead one phase** — framing, roofing, joinery, plaster — then hands to the next.

Why this beats the apprenticeship model for this purpose:
- **A far smaller ask.** "Teach for six weeks" is a completely different proposition from "take an apprentice for three years." The second is why succession has failed.
- **Genuine specialists.** Each phase is led by someone who is actually best at it.
- **It scales to a register** rather than depending on individual heroism.
- **Paid teaching work** for people at the end of their working lives who have no successor and would otherwise take the knowledge with them.

### The second output nobody is counting

Film it. Write it down.

The programme produces **a documented technique archive** as a byproduct — and that archive is valuable whether or not any particular house gets finished. It also reaches heritage and cultural-preservation funding that housing alone cannot, which means the archive can subsidise the build.

> This is the clearest expression yet of the era-town / craft-transmission argument in `musubu_archetype_axes.md`. Traditional building technique is held by a shrinking, aging population. Apprentices who moved there specifically to learn is a far better story than retro shopping — and a real answer to *who maintains the akiya stock* across the whole model.

## 4. The Four-Way Structure

| Party | Brings | Gets |
|---|---|---|
| **Musubu** | Demand coordination, the map, cohort formation, government interface | The renovation-cost problem solved; a recruitment magnet; an operator who knows how to run a building |
| **Oak House** | Operations, tenant funnel, brand, rotation mechanic | Near-zero-cost inventory, a differentiated product, network value, a story |
| **Carpenter register** | Technique, licensed supervision, legitimacy | Successors, paid teaching, legacy, documentation |
| **Municipality** | Akiya access, subsidy, building permission | Renovated stock, residents, trades that stay in town |

## 5. The Loop

This is the part that makes it a system rather than three good ideas.

```
Cohort 1 arrives to learn carpentry
        ↓
Cohort 1 builds the houses
        ↓
Cohort 2 moves into those houses
        ↓
Cohort 1 now holds a certified skill in the availability register
        ↓
Next town needs a renovation crew → the register already knows who they are and when they're free
        ↓
Cohort 1 (or some of them) circulates onward — or stays and teaches Cohort 3
```

**Each cohort trains the next cohort's builders.** The circulating-labour layer stops depending on skilled people happening to show up and starts generating its own supply. It is the first genuinely self-feeding loop in the model.

See `musubu_living_profile.md` §"The Availability Register" for the matching side.

## 6. Funding Alignment

Against the programmes in `05_Government_and_Policy/musubu_government_alignment.md`:

| Programme | Fits |
|---|---|
| Workforce reskilling (¥1T / 5yr) | Trainee stipends and certification — this is literally a reskilling programme |
| Regional Revitalization 2.0 | Akiya conversion and population inflow |
| 地域おこし協力隊 | Programme coordinator and the steward role |
| Heritage / cultural preservation | The technique archive |
| Vocational training (職業訓練) | Formal certification pathway |

→ *All require verification of current terms via `japanese-research` before use.*

**Note the unusual position this creates:** the programme has a legitimate claim on reskilling, revitalization, *and* cultural-preservation budgets simultaneously, because it genuinely does all three. That is rare and worth building the government pitch around.

## Parked — Legal and Structural

*Per `07_Ideas_and_Future/musubu_future_development_notes.md`.*

- **建築基準法 compliance** for work performed by trainees
- **Liability and insurance** — who carries it when an unlicensed trainee's work later fails
- **建築士 licensing** requirements for structural alteration
- Employment-law status of trainees: are they students, workers, or volunteers? This determines stipend treatment, insurance, and visa eligibility for non-Japanese participants
- Whether "sweat equity" creates an ownership claim that needs formal structuring, and what happens if someone leaves mid-build

## Open Questions

- Would Oak House actually entertain deal shape B, or does listing a property they don't operate dilute their brand promise?
- What's the realistic labour share of a Japanese akiya renovation? **The single most important number to obtain.**
- How many master carpenters would realistically say yes to a six-week teaching residency, and what would it pay?
- Does a trainee who leaves after three months keep any claim?
- Can non-Japanese participants legally join on a training basis, and under which status? (Directly relevant to the multicultural argument in `musubu_eldercare_wellness_town.md`.)
- Does the sweat-equity house end up cheaper but slower — and if so, does the timeline break cohort synchrony?
