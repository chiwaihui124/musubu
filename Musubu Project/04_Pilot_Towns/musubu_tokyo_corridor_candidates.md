# Tokyo-Corridor Pilot Candidates
## Towns near the population you're actually trying to move

*Written 9 August 2026. Companion to `musubu_pilot_town_analysis.md`, which is not superseded — it answered a different question. That document optimised for government receptiveness, model fit and replication potential. **Proximity to the source population was never scored.** This one adds that axis.*

---

## Why this document exists

The existing tier list is Bungotakada (Ōita), Namie (Fukushima), Imabari (Ehime), with Yuzawa (Niigata) in tier 3. Two of the top three sit in Kyushu and Shikoku — a long way from the Tokyo 23 wards, which is the population the whole project is trying to move.

**Demand already points the other way.** The 2024 relocation-interest rankings put **Gunma first, Shizuoka second, Tochigi third** — all Shinkansen-commutable from Tokyo. People who want to leave Tokyo overwhelmingly want to land within reach of it.

*Provenance: derived — 2024 移住希望地ランキング as reported; get the primary 「ふるさと回帰支援センター」 release before quoting.*

---

## The eligibility door nobody uses

移住支援金 requires five of the past ten years in the Tokyo 23 wards (most recent year continuous), and a destination **outside 東京圏 — or a designated 条件不利地域 within it.**

Tokyo Prefecture contains eligible destinations. On the mainland: **奥多摩町 Okutama** and **檜原村 Hinohara**. Plus the islands (大島, 利島, 新島, 神津島, 三宅, 御蔵島, 八丈, 青ヶ島, 小笠原).

> **A family can move house, collect up to ¥1M plus ¥1M per child, and still be in Tokyo.**

*Provenance: municipal — 内閣府 地方創生 published 条件不利地域 list.*

## Candidate profiles

### 奥多摩町 Okutama — Tokyo Prefecture

| | |
|---|---|
| **Population** | ~5,200 *(13,000+ in the 1960s)* |
| **Aging rate** | 46.9% |
| **Status** | 消滅可能性都市 — projected to disappear by 2040 |
| **To central Tokyo** | ~2 hrs by train (Ome → Chuo line) |
| **Already offers** | Akiya bank since 2014 · a separate **youth** akiya bank · a **¥0 akiya bank** · settlement subsidy to ¥1M · akiya renovation subsidy to ¥2M |
| **In-migrants** | roughly **10% of the population** |

**Why it's the strongest first pilot.** The incentives already exist and already work — a bit. People do move to Okutama; the town gives houses away to get them. That makes it the cleanest possible test of the actual thesis, because a pilot there varies **one** thing: *does arriving together beat arriving one at a time?* You cannot confuse the result with "the cash worked," because the cash is already a control condition.

Contrast Bungotakada, where a pilot would introduce coordination, incentives and a brand-new institutional relationship simultaneously — three variables, one experiment.

**The line that does work in a room:** a place inside Tokyo Metropolis that the government projects will cease to exist.

**Watch for:** forestry-collapse town in a steep valley — buildable land is genuinely scarce, which constrains the co-living and cluster tiles. Check this before committing.

### 檜原村 Hinohara — Tokyo Prefecture

| | |
|---|---|
| **Population** | **2,038** (2023) |
| **Aging rate** | 52%+ |
| **Status** | 消滅可能性都市 |
| **Access** | No railway. Bus from Musashi-Itsukaichi |
| **Already offers** | Relocation grant **¥1M single / ¥1.5M household** · 定住促進サポート事業 support for those working, starting a business, or **teleworking** in the village |

**The interesting anomaly: demand exceeds supply.** Reporting notes that migration applicants keep coming and **the akiya supply can't keep up** — 「空き家の供給も追いつかない」. That is the inverse of the standard rural problem and it flips what Musubu would be for. Here the constraint isn't recruiting people; it's **releasing housing stock**, which is `famichi_akiya_strategy` / housing-coordination territory, not demand generation.

**Strategically valuable as a contrast case.** If a village of 2,000 with no train line has a housing-supply bottleneck, the binding constraint in this corridor may be supply, not demand — which would be a significant correction to the project's core assumption. **Worth confirming early, because it's cheap to check and expensive to be wrong about.**

### 湯沢町 Yuzawa — Niigata *(currently tier 3)*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Population** | ~8,000 |
| **To Tokyo** | **77 min by Shinkansen** |
| **Housing** | **57 resort condo buildings, ~15,000 units.** Post-bubble collapse gave them a 「負動産」 reputation; units have sold from around **¥100,000** |
| **Trend** | Households **increasing since 2020**; permanent residents rising across all age groups |
| **Already offers** | Relocation grant ¥1M couple / ¥600k single (via the prefecture's job-matching route) · rent subsidy **¥30,000/month for up to 2 years** · contract-cost subsidy to ¥120,000 |

**Badly underrated on the old criteria.** Yuzawa is the only candidate with **housing stock already at scale** — 15,000 units sitting in a town of 8,000 people. Every other town on every list has an akiya problem measured in dozens. Yuzawa's is measured in thousands, already built, already serviced, already connected to Tokyo in 77 minutes.

For a mechanic that depends on *many people arriving at once*, this is the only place in Japan where the housing constraint simply isn't one. **A 200-household cohort could be housed here next year.** Nowhere else on any tier list can say that.

**Watch for:** resort condos are studio-to-small and were built for weekends — management fees, repair reserves and aging infrastructure are real. Family suitability needs checking, though renovation examples exist. And the town's existing dynamic is second-home owners converting to permanent, which is a *different* social fabric from a cohort arriving together.

### 南牧村 Nanmoku — Gunma

| | |
|---|---|
| **Population** | **1,440** *(down 500+ in a decade)* |
| **Aging rate** | **68.2%** — highest in Japan |
| **Status** | Ranked **worst in the country** as a 消滅可能性自治体 in both 2014 (−89.9%) and the 2024 re-verification |
| **Already offers** | 南牧山村ぐらし支援協議会 — an established migration support council |

**Too far gone for a first pilot. Powerful as a second.**

One genuinely striking finding: **the over-75s in Nanmoku show *lower* frailty rates than comparable areas.** A village that is two-thirds elderly is producing healthier old age than the national norm. That is a direct empirical hook for `musubu_eldercare_wellness_town.md`, and it inverts the expected story — the eldercare archetype has usually been framed as a service to be delivered *to* a declining place, not something that place is already doing well.

*Provenance: derived — reported private-body survey. Find the primary source; if it holds, it's one of the most quotable facts in the corpus.*

## The corridor principle

Distribution should not be geographic-aesthetic — one per region, evenly spaced on a map. Japanese domestic migration runs in **channels**: Tōhoku, Kōshin'etsu and northern Kantō feed Tokyo; Shikoku, Chūgoku and Hokuriku feed Osaka; Kyushu feeds Fukuoka.

**A return flow runs back down the same road it came up.** Pilot towns should sit on the **return leg of an existing outflow corridor**, because:

- the mover often has a pre-existing tie to the region — which also makes the 関係人口 eligibility test far easier to satisfy
- transport already exists, so the way back is cheap — directly reducing §S1's cost and its psychological weight
- family visits in both directions stay viable, which is the quiet blocker nobody lists on a survey

This is checkable before any survey runs: 総務省's 住民基本台帳人口移動報告 publishes prefecture-to-prefecture flows. **The corridors are already measured.**

## The inversion — let the demand test pick the towns

The current sequence is *choose a town → recruit people into it*. That is the sequence every failed regional-revitalisation programme has used, and it contradicts the product's own thesis.

**Invert it.** The instruments already exist — `musubu_demand_test.md` and `musubu_intake_instrument.md` (the older `musubu_national_survey.md` is archived). Add a geography question with candidate regions, run it once nationally, and see where demand actually clusters.

> **The first threshold isn't a town's. It's the survey's.** Whichever candidate region's cluster clears first becomes pilot one.

This is the same mechanic the product runs on, applied to the project's own biggest decision: people declare conditionally, and the thing that clears the bar is the thing that happens. It also produces the answer to *"how many people would move from Tokyo toward Fukuoka?"* as a by-product rather than a separate research task — and it turns the pilot selection from a judgment call into evidence, which is a very different thing to put in front of a municipality.

**Concrete next move:** add one question to the demand test offering 5–8 candidate regions with honest travel times, and let people pick more than one. Costs nothing. Runs before any town has been approached.

## Open questions

1. **Is the binding constraint in this corridor demand or supply?** Hinohara suggests supply. If that generalises, the project's core assumption needs adjusting. Cheapest possible check.
2. **Does Okutama have buildable land?** Steep forestry valley — constrains co-living and cluster tiles.
3. **Are Yuzawa's resort units family-viable** after fees and renovation, or singles/couples only?
4. **Verify the Nanmoku frailty finding.** If real, it's a headline for the eldercare archetype.
5. **Pull the actual migration-flow matrix** from 住民基本台帳人口移動報告 and map the corridors properly, rather than reasoning from geography.
