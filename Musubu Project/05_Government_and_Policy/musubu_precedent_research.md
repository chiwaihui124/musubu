# Precedent Research
## What Japan has already tried, what failed, and where Musubu actually sits

*Written August 2026. First pass — desk research from secondary sources. **Every figure here needs primary-source verification before use in a pitch.** Several came from consultancy blogs and commercial sites rather than government publications.*

---

## Why this document exists

The first question any informed investor, ministry official or town assembly will ask is:

> **"Japan has been doing this for a decade. Why hasn't it worked, and why would yours?"**

Until now the project had no answer. It does now, and the answer is stronger than expected.

---

## 1. The state has effectively conceded that 地方創生 failed

Launched in 2014 in response to the report projecting 896 municipalities toward extinction. A decade on:

- **Tokyo's net in-migration was 68,285 in 2023** — unchanged even through COVID. The policy's central objective, correcting Tokyo concentration, did not happen.
- Academic assessment is that structural problems — shrinking rural labour force, deteriorating production base — remain fundamentally unresolved, with little evidence that place-based approaches worked in practice.
- Critics describe the policy as political rhetoric rather than reality.

**Implication for Musubu:** you are not competing against a working system. You are entering a field where the incumbent approach has publicly failed, which lowers the bar for "why should we try something else" considerably.

---

## 2. The diagnosis in the literature *is* Musubu's thesis

**This is the most important finding in this document.**

The most-cited explanation for why regional revitalization failed:

> It attempted to solve what is fundamentally an **adaptive problem** as a **technical problem** — government focused on subsidies and facility construction rather than on changing regional values, relationships and ways of living.

That is the coordination argument, arrived at independently by people evaluating a decade of failure. Musubu's central claim — *this is not a money problem, it is a coordination and social-formation problem* — is **corroborated by the critique literature rather than merely asserted by the project.**

Use this. It converts the thesis from "here is my clever idea" into "here is what the people who studied the failure concluded, and here is a mechanism that acts on it."

### The measurable version of the same failure
Over **1,200 michi-no-eki (roadside stations)** were built as revitalization symbols. Fewer than **30% draw over a million visitors a year**, against roughly ¥1B construction cost each and tens of millions in annual maintenance. Building a facility and hoping people come is a documented, expensive failure mode.

### And a direct warning for Musubu
Reported as the standardised *"grammar of regional revitalization"*: subsidy dependence, consultant outsourcing, and **copying successful cases from other regions without local history or culture**. Meanwhile the mechanisms for autonomous local economies never spread.

**A single Musubu template applied to fourteen towns is precisely this failure mode.** It validates the instinct that each town declares its own direction and carries its own identity (`musubu_portal_taxonomy.md`), and it means uniformity is a risk to be actively designed against, not an efficiency to pursue.

## 3. 地域おこし協力隊 is the strongest evidence *for* the model — and shows exactly what's missing

The closest existing thing to what Musubu does: place a person, support them, give them a role.

| | |
|---|---|
| Members in post (FY2023) | **6,447** |
| **Settle in the region after their term** | **~80%** |
| **Experience post-arrival mismatch** | **~50%** |

**Read those two numbers together, because that is the whole argument.**

**80% settlement proves supported, structured placement works.** That is a remarkable retention figure and it is the empirical foundation Musubu has been missing. The policy claim — that coordinated arrival retains better than individual relocation — has partial evidence already sitting in a government programme.

**50% mismatch is the gap Musubu fills.** The documented causes are: ambiguous job descriptions, a large gap between the posting and the reality, isolation from existing residents, and income insecurity when the term ends.

Every one of those is a matching-and-coordination failure, and every one has a Musubu answer:
- Ambiguity → the intake instrument and the declared-direction town profile
- Posting-vs-reality gap → the live feed and the trial visit
- Isolation from residents → simultaneous arrival, the commons, the resident view
- Income cliff at term end → the availability register and the circulating-cohort layer

> **The pitch line:** *"Japan already has a programme that retains 80% of the people it places, and half of them arrive to find it wasn't what they were told. We are not inventing placement. We are fixing the matching."*

## 4. The national relocation subsidy is much smaller than it sounds

移住支援金 — up to ¥1M per household, ¥600K single, for moving out of the Tokyo area with employment or a business.

| | Cases | People |
|---|---|---|
| FY2022 | 2,495 | 5,108 |
| FY2023 | 3,542 | 7,782 |

**That is the flagship national programme, across all of Japan, in a year.**

Two things follow:

**Scale reframe.** The 2032 vision has 176 households arriving in one town over six years. Against a national programme moving ~3,500 households a year, that is roughly 5% of national annual volume, **concentrated in a single municipality**. What looked modest is actually significant, and the concentration is the entire point — 3,500 households spread across 1,700 municipalities is two per town, which produces nothing.

**Cost benchmark.** The state is spending on the order of ¥3.5B a year through this channel and Tokyo's net in-migration has not moved. That is the number to put beside any Musubu cost-per-retained-household figure.

## 5. Nobody has done voluntary coordinated migration

Searching Japanese sources for 集団移住 / group migration returns two things, and neither is what Musubu proposes:

**Disaster relocation** — 防災集団移転促進事業, running since 1972, moving whole settlements out of hazard zones with resident consent and subsidised moving costs:

| Event | Households moved |
|---|---|
| 1972 Kyushu floods, Ryūgatake, Kumamoto | 329 |
| 1983 Miyake-jima eruption | 301 |
| 1991 Unzen-Fugendake eruption | 124 |
| 1993 Okushiri tsunami | 55 |
| 2000 Usu-zan eruption | 152 |
| 2004 Chūetsu earthquake | 115 |

**Historical emigration** — the state-organised Brazil and Paraguay programmes, ended 1993.

### The reframe this gives you

**Japan already knows how to move three hundred households together. It has a legal framework, a subsidy mechanism, and decades of practice. It has only ever done it when a volcano erupted.**

That is a genuinely strong pitch line, and it does two useful things at once: it proves the mechanism is administratively possible in Japan, and it makes the novelty precise. Musubu is not proposing something unprecedented. It is proposing to do voluntarily, and for opportunity, what Japan has repeatedly done under duress.

**It also means there may be an existing legal and administrative scaffold worth studying** — 防災集団移転促進事業 has solved consent procedures, group land acquisition and relocation funding. Whether any of it transfers is an open question and a good one for a Japanese-speaking researcher.

## 5b. Nagareyama — a town that grew, without coordination

*Added August 2026, surfaced via a NotebookLM pass over the corpus. Verify before use.*

**Nagareyama City, Chiba: reported >20% population growth**, driven by childcare and transit design — notably a childcare shuttle service that let commuting parents drop children at a station-side facility rather than at distant nurseries, plus sustained family-focused investment.

**This is the most useful comparison case the project has, and it complicates our story in a productive way.**

It is a genuine counter-example: a Japanese municipality that reversed decline **without any coordination mechanism at all** — no threshold, no cohort, no synchronised arrival. It simply removed a specific, well-identified friction for a specific segment, at scale, over years.

**Two things follow, and both are worth having answers to before a pitch:**

1. **"Why don't you just do what Nagareyama did?"** is a question that will be asked, and the honest answer is that Nagareyama had commuter-rail proximity to Tokyo, which almost none of the 896 at-risk municipalities have. Its playbook does not transfer to a town two hours from Fukuoka with no passenger railway. **But saying that requires knowing it.**
2. **It supports the blocker thesis more than it threatens the coordination thesis.** Nagareyama identified one binding constraint for one segment and removed it — which is precisely what the blocker dataset is supposed to enable at national scale. It is evidence for the *diagnostic* half of the model even as it bypasses the coordination half.

→ *Research task: confirm the growth figure and period, and establish how much is attributable to rail proximity versus policy.*

## 5c. 結 with a ledger already ran for thirty years: ふれあい切符

*Added September 2026. Verified against secondary sources on 2026-09-17; see `musubu_coordination_theory.md` §9 for the argument and the source list.*

**Fureai Kippu ("ticket for a caring relationship")** is a Japanese time-credit system for elder care: an hour of help earns an hour of credit, redeemable later or transferable to a parent elsewhere. Lineage from Teruko Mizushima's Volunteer Labour Bank (Osaka, 1973); modern form created 1995 by the Sawayaka Welfare Foundation; reported at roughly **400 branches and 70,000 active participants**. **Tier 3 figures.**

Two things follow. The "結 with a ledger" framing on the concept page is not rhetorical: Japan built one at scale. And the failure mode matters: the 2000 Long-Term Care Insurance Act let people buy care with cash, and paid volunteers left the system. **A state cash channel crowded out reciprocal credit.** Any Musubu reciprocity layer denominated in something a subsidy can substitute for is fragile by the same mechanism; standing and vouching (registry #15) are harder to crowd out than redeemable credits.

## 5d. Broad listening has a Japanese precedent with a seat in the Diet

*Added September 2026. Electoral facts verified 2026-09-17 (Jiji, Nikkei); Tier 2. Characterisation of the method is Tier 3.*

**安野貴博 (Takahiro Anno)** ran for Tokyo governor in 2024 on a manifesto built by **ブロードリスニング**: AI clustering of open citizen input (the Pol.is / Talk to the City family), receiving about 150,000 votes and the grand prix of the 19th Manifesto Awards. He founded **チームみらい (Team Mirai)** in May 2025; in the July 2025 House of Councillors election it won one proportional seat and over 2% of the proportional vote, meeting the legal requirements for party status.

**Implication:** the corpus's "asking properly is only recently possible" argument (`musubu_ai_enablement.md`) is no longer an argument in Japan. It is a precedent. The loop drawn on `Portal/musubu-coordination-layer.html` (people say what is in the way, it becomes a legible number, somebody acts on it) has been run as a political method and has produced a legislator. This is recorded as precedent, not as a partnership strategy.

**Design consequence:** the intake instrument should rank blockers by **breadth across segments**, as Pol.is surfaces bridging statements, not by raw count. A blocker named by 23-year-olds, young parents and retirees alike is the one a municipality can remove with broad support.

## 6. The competitive landscape is inventory, not coordination

| Who | What | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **LIFULL HOME'S / At Home** | National akiya bank aggregator | Selected by MLIT tender, 2017. The incumbents. |
| **Akiya Japan** | Aggregates ~595 sources, English | Directory |
| **Akiya Banks** | US-based, English, curated listings | Launched 2021. Directory with a human layer. |
| **Old Houses Japan** | Akiya bank directory | Directory |
| **Municipal akiya banks** | ~1,700 municipalities, most operate one | Matchmaking between owners and buyers |
| **ADDress** | Subscription multi-location living, Gaiax-backed | Launched 2019. **The closest thing to a network model.** |

**Every one of these lists houses. None of them assemble people.**

That is Musubu's competitive position stated in one line, and it is defensible — because coordination requires demand-side liquidity that a listing site structurally cannot accumulate.

### Civic-tech platforms worth studying
Not competitors, but solved problems the town portal will otherwise re-solve badly:

- **Decidim** — open-source participatory democracy platform, used at municipal scale (Barcelona and others). Directly relevant to the town portal's governance and voting surfaces, and to the question of who may participate in a decision.
- **Loomio** — collaborative decision-making with a durable record of how a group reached a conclusion. Relevant to co-op governance rather than to the public portal.
- **Pol.is / vTaiwan** — the Taiwanese consensus-finding process (Audrey Tang's team) that clusters opinions and surfaces the statements that *bridge* clusters rather than the ones that win a majority. The direct model for how the blocker mirror should rank what it shows first. See §5d.

**Both are mature, open-source, and have already faced the moderation, legitimacy and participation-inequality problems the portal will run into.** Worth a serious look before building any governance feature from scratch.

### ADDress deserves a proper look
Subscription access to a network of shared houses across Japan, with movement between them as the core product. **That is the rotation mechanic proposed for the Oak House partnership, already built and operating.** It may be a better partner than Oak House for exactly that reason — or a competitor if it moves toward settlement rather than nomadism. Either way, `musubu_build_partnership_model.md` should not name a partner before someone has studied ADDress properly.

## 7. Your "why now" answer, sharpened

The founder's answer to why this can work when a decade of policy didn't: **AI**, and specifically not as an efficiency but as an *access* change.

The strongest form of it, which belongs in the pitch:

> An eighty-year-old in a depopulating town has never been a user of civic technology — not because they don't care, but because every tool ever built for them required navigating an interface. Conversation removes that. If the majority population of a town can participate by talking, then the people who usually get consulted last, or not at all, become participants.

**This answers a criticism as well as a "why now."** The literature's diagnosis is that revitalization failed by treating an adaptive problem technically — and a large part of *why* it stayed technical is that the tools only ever reached the administrators and the incomers. Voice interfaces are the first plausible route to including the residents whose values and consent actually determine whether any of this works.

It also connects to the project's least-treated risk: existing-resident opposition (`07_Ideas_and_Future`). The people most likely to kill a pilot are the ones current tools cannot reach.

1. **The pitch gains its missing slide.** Not "here's a good idea" but "here's what a decade of failure concluded, here's the programme that already retains 80%, and here's the specific gap."
2. **The scale question is resolved.** 176 households in one town is ~5% of national annual relocation-subsidy volume, concentrated. That's the argument for density over spread.
3. **Uniformity is now a named risk**, corroborated by the record.
4. **ADDress must be researched before any partnership doc names Oak House.**
5. **防災集団移転 may offer transferable legal scaffolding** for group relocation.
