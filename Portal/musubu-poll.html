Nobody wants to be the only person who moved.

Roughly nine hundred Japanese towns are on a path toward disappearing. The usual explanation is that people prefer cities. We think that is the wrong diagnosis, and that the real problem is something much more fixable.

Ask people whether they would live somewhere quieter, with time and space and a house they could afford, and a great many say yes. Ask them to actually go, and almost nobody does. Not because they were lying — because there are around 1,700 municipalities and you cannot tell, from outside, which one has work you could do, which one is quietly emptying, or whether you would end up the only new person there for a decade.

Tokyo is the only option with a known shape. Everyone can picture it. A town you have never lived in is not really an option — it is a guess, and people are right to refuse a guess.

What we mean by coordination

Nobody moves unless everybody moves.

What this adds is not persuasion. It is specification — turning one town into something you can actually weigh against Tokyo, because you know who is coming, what the work pays, when it happens, and exactly what occurs if it falls through. The mechanism is not complicated. It already works for funding things that do not exist yet; we are pointing it at moving somewhere instead.

  1. A town says what it is short of. Not a brochure — specifics. Two teachers. Six people who will learn a trade. Families with young children, and here is the housing we are holding.
  2. People commit conditionally. You say: I will go, if fifty other households go. Your deposit is held. Nothing happens yet.
  3. Everyone watches the number. It is public. You can see it move. You can see who else is in — how many families, what people do, roughly who your neighbours would be.
  4. You get to know each other first. Months before anyone moves: forums, trial weekends, meeting up. The community forms before the migration, not after.
  5. The threshold is met, or it is not. If it fills, everyone goes in the same season. If it does not, everyone gets their money back and nothing was risked.

Arriving with fifty other households is a completely different experience from arriving alone. Nobody is already inside. Nobody is the odd one out. It is the closest an adult gets to the first week of university — which, for most people, was the most friendship-dense year of their life.

Why now, and not ten years ago

Japan has spent a decade and enormous sums on regional revitalization. The most common conclusion from people who studied why it did not work: it treated a social problem as a technical one — subsidies and buildings, rather than the conditions under which people actually change their lives.

There is also one existing programme that does place people in rural towns with support, and roughly eight in ten of them stay. It works. It is just small, and about half of the people placed arrive to find the reality did not match what they were told. That is a matching problem, and matching thousands of people well is a thing that only recently became possible.

What this page is

Two things at once, and we would rather say so.

The first is a genuine question — would this change what you would do? Everything anyone submits gets published back as numbers. That is the point of asking.

The second is an open call. This project does not have a team yet. If you think it could work, or think it could not and know why, or you have the skills and the language it needs — that is at least as valuable as your survey answer. There is a section for it at the end.

What happens now

Everything gets published back as numbers, updated as they come in — including the answers from people who think this will not work, because a page showing only agreement is an advertisement. If you left an email you will hear what came of it. If you offered to help, someone will actually write to you.

Prototype of the instrument, not a live survey.
