Nine hundred Japanese towns are on track to disappear. Here is an idea, and I would like to know what is wrong with it.

About three minutes to read. Nothing to sign up for.

Ask people whether they would live somewhere quieter, with more time and a house they could afford, and a lot of them say yes. Almost nobody goes.

I do not think that is because people prefer cities. I think it is because Tokyo is the only option with a shape you can picture. There are around 1,700 municipalities and you cannot tell, from outside, which one has work you could do, which one is quietly emptying, or whether you would end up being the only new person there for ten years. A town you cannot judge is not really an option. It is a guess, and people are right to refuse a guess.

This does not exist. No town has agreed to anything, there is no company, and nobody is being recruited. It is an idea I have been working on, and I am at the point where I need people to tell me what is wrong with it before I go any further.

The idea

  1. A town says what it is short of — not a brochure. Two teachers. Six people who will learn a building trade. Families with young children, and here is the housing we would hold for them.
  2. People commit conditionally. You say: I will go, if thirty other households go. A deposit is held. Nothing happens yet.
  3. The number is public. You can watch it move, and see roughly who else is in — how many families, what people do, who your neighbours would be.
  4. You meet before you move. Months of it. Visits, meals, questions. The community forms first, and the moving happens after.
  5. It fills, or it does not. If it fills, everyone goes in the same season. If it does not, the money goes back and nobody has moved anywhere.

The point is not to persuade anyone that the countryside is nice. It is that arriving somewhere with thirty other households is a completely different thing from arriving alone — nobody is already on the inside, and nobody is the only new person.

What it would look like

A town of about five thousand people. A school, a clinic, a shop, work you could actually do. Rent around ¥38,000 for a whole house.

Thirty households arrive the same spring. Six of them are learning carpentry from local builders who are close to retiring and have no successors. Someone opens a café in a shopfront that has been empty for eleven years, because forty households have already said they would be customers. A kindergarten that closed in 2019 reopens, because there are suddenly enough children.

Nobody's commute is more than ten minutes.

What I am actually asking

Not whether you would move. I need the version you would say to someone else, not the version you would say to me. If you think it is naive, that is the most useful thing you could tell me.

  1. Can you explain back what you think this is?
    If it does not survive being explained in one sentence, nothing else matters.
  2. What is the first thing that seems wrong, or naive?
    Whatever came to mind while reading. That first reaction is the useful one.
  3. Who does this remind you of?
    Someone you know who might actually want this — or someone who would hate it.
  4. Which words feel wrong?
    Anything that sounds foreign, or like marketing, or like it was written by someone who does not live here.
